Case 01 · FYNDNA · Fraud & document risk

Eight steps to close one risk.
I got it to four.

Risk reviewers check every document in a loan application for anything that doesn’t add up. The flow they were handed was borrowed from a different feature. This is how I redesigned it around the one decision they make all day.

02The setting

Before a bank lends you money, someone reads your paperwork.

That someone usually works for a Credit Information Company (CIC): a third party the bank pays to judge how risky your documents are. FYNDNA builds the tool they do it in.

  1. You

    apply for a loan and upload payslips, statements, ID.

  2. The bank

    sends the application out for a credit check.

  3. A reviewer

    goes through it document by document, risk by risk.

  4. A decision

    moves the application forward, or stops it.

05Who decides

Four kinds of reviewers make that call.

Different jobs, same screen. Each team I worked with spoke for one of them.

01

Agency verifiers

External agencies who check a customer’s CIC/CIBIL records on the bank’s behalf.

02

Underwriters & credit officers

Match CIC/CIBIL data against the bank’s risk policy to set eligibility and interest rates.

03

Fraud risk analysts

Watch credit reports for application fraud, identity theft and compromised profiles.

04

Relationship managers

Pull a preliminary report, with consent, to surface pre-approved offers in branch.

06The brief

There’s an existing flow similar to this. Just finish it off.

— the brief, more or less in full
07My read

The old flow was too flexible for a job that never changes.

What the old flow was for

Comparing anything with anything: a document against a dashboard, another module, any section.

What reviewers actually do

The same call, risk after risk, hundreds of times a day.

Flexibility helps when you’re exploring. Here, every extra option was just another click.

08The old flow

So I walked it, click by click.

Closing a single risk took eight steps. The redesign does it in four.

9–10documents per reviewer, per day
3–4risks to resolve in each document
8steps for every one of those risks

11Dilemma 1 of 3

Where does the work happen?

An overlay, or a page of its own?

I was asked to reuse the slide-out overlay from another module, for consistency. But reviewers make a call that every later decision depends on. They need the document and its risks in front of them, not a panel over a dashboard.

What I learned

Consistency matters, until it costs the person using the screen.

12Dilemma 2 of 3

What does one risk look like?

How much should a risk card say?

A risk is either untouched or resolved, and the card has to make that obvious. I tried hierarchy after hierarchy with the other teams, to learn which details actually mattered.

Some of the explorations: full cards, compact rows, radio buttons, always-on buttons.

The one that worked

The one that worked

Key facts first, actions on hover. No wall of identical buttons, and the reviewer reads the risk before acting on it.

  1. 1Cure
  2. 2Cannot cure
  3. 3Verify

Every team agreed all three carry equal weight, so none is styled as primary.

What I learned

Iteration only pays off with steady feedback. It’s what told me which changes mattered.

13Dilemma 3 of 3

How do you see the whole application?

Sort the dashboard by document, or by risk?

Teams wanted different groupings. I built the options as dashboard widgets and took them back to each team to rank.

Widget options: by document, by data source, by cross-validation, and as a table.

By document, with the risks inside

By document, with the risks inside

The dashboard tells reviewers what they’re walking into before they open a single risk.

  1. 1Filters on top
  2. 2Risks grouped under each document
  3. 3A status: cured or pending

Reviewers find and narrow what they need without holding the whole application in their head.

14Looking back

What I’d tell the next designer.

Build the wrong version first. Once the flawed flow is on screen, it argues against itself.

Eight steps didn’t become four because the screens got cleaner. They did because the problem got reframed.

Getting three teams with conflicting asks behind one direction was the real work. It just doesn’t screenshot well.
